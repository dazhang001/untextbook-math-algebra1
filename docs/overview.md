# An Overview: "Un-Textbook"-ing Mathematics
---

{: .note}
> Don't worry! Not all overviews (especially pertaining to the math content) will be this long. Just wanted us to start off on the right foot, and you will see sidebars just like this every now and again unrelated specifically to math, but to reinforce positive and productive ways to think. 
>
> Remember: culture, commitment, confidence! :)

You're soon to enter the realm of the abstract.

No more just...

$$\displaylines{ 1+1=2 \\ 3\times 7 = 21 }$$

And more...

$$\displaylines{\begin{cases} 2x+4y=7 \\ 7x+15y=232 \end{cases} }$$

{: .text-center}
...or...

$$\displaylines{S_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}k=\frac{(n+1)(n)}{2}}$$

{: .text-center}
...or...

$$x^2+10x+21=(x+7)(x+3)$$

Scary, huh? Letters, notation, fancy symbols. Math is full of it from here on out. Here's a bunch in tier-list form, because we all enjoy a tier-list...

![Math Symbol Tierlist](units\overview_img\math_sym_tierlist.png)

I'm of the opinion that math, drowned in the days of drill & kill and muscle/rote memory problem solving, gets this unfair and often implicit characteristic early on that it's just about **"numbers"**. 

And then we act so surprised when we see a big drop off in mathematical achievement coming into high school level work when things start going from concrete (numbers) to the abstract.

Humans, including yourself, are creatures of habit after all. We see similar ideas across years in our early childhood, reinforcing after countless number problems solved and right (or wrong) answers gotten that math is, ultimately, a game of calculation using numbers. I mean, what else can we expect when how we assess in math is zeroed-in on solving...

> *"...if Adam has 3 apples and Susan has 2257 apples, how big does the container have to be on the back of a truck to hold all their apples together given an average diameter of 3 inches per apple?"*

And even I would find that problem more intellectually stimulating than just "how many would they have altogether?".

Is it entirely wrong that we need to grind through endless calculations in our youth? **Not quite**.

**Consistency** builds **familiarity and persistence**, and that builds **competency and confidence**, which in turn builds **success and self-motivation**. I've always been on this train that we do need a lot of practice to get good at something. Every great coach would tell you this. Teachers are should strive to be just like coaches. Make things hard in practice, so that when game time comes around, everything seems to be much easier. Math is such an unforgiving "sport". You slow down for even a second and you can find the train leaving you. It's why we memorize multiplication tables and grind through addition and subtraction problems at a young age. Practice hard, make everything easier later. Repetition gives into muscle memory, thus taking away from needing working memory (the capacity for your brain to process information) that can be used to address the other complexities of a given problem.

And here's the kicker, textbooks won't tell you any of what was said before, or what I'm about to say after. They're emotionless. They present information in a perfect rational manner, with assumptions that the students who read them have all the prerequisite knowledge needed for their current level. Textbooks are perfected under countless revision, all organized and neat. 

**We humans aren't like textbooks**. We want instant gratification. We act irrationally. We take the path of least resistance when it favors us - not because of the benefit of taking such a path, but because of the cost of not taking it (e.g. the delay of gratification/satisfaction in completing a problem which is thousands of times more cathartic than wanting something immediately, wanting to go through the meticulous work of calculating and writing out steps). Not every one of us has perfect memory, nor has developed a good system of thinking about how to solve a problem because that's harder work for teachers at earlier levels. Math education asks you to memorize a set of algorithms to perform rather than gaining true understanding of "why?". 

We need to be okay with the idea that we might not get it at first, but we need to make the effort and attempt to continue to ask and answer this question of "why"?

As I write, my attempt is to make this **read more like a book, or a set of messy notes**, rather than the perfection of a textbook. A textbook that assumes steps to be self-evident to the reader. That doesn't go through the messy work of what it actually might feel like solving one of their problems. That it reads more **"human"**. That when I do a problem, I explain my thought process and always answer this question of "why?". That understanding the ability to reason and answer "why?" will serve useful for anyone in their life, because justification and defense is a hallmark of great learning and growth - not just in math, but in everything you'll touch and interact with for the rest of your life.

And with that being said, I must make one thing very clear:

{: .important}
> I might not see things how you see them. I might not even be the most perfect "math teacher" there is in terms of mastery of the content.
>
> And I think that's okay.
>
> I want you to get a real human taste as to what it might look like going through my brain as I try to synthesize, instruct, and go over these topics at a **very human level**. How to think, how to reason, how to walk through steps without fear for the costs of messing up. We can strive to be perfect, but we'll never be perfect.
>
> That's what "un-textbook"-ing is all about. 

The aim here is to give you a math resource, that hopefully and by design, doesn't read like it was produced by the most intelligent people in the math community. That it isn't formal to the point of disturbingly robotic. That it weaves a bit of humor while still being grounded. That it shows how I think, how you might think, and everything in between so that you achieve a level of understanding that isn't just memorizing algorithms (specific steps to do a problem), but rather have the ability and confidence to present and work through a problem like a great teacher would.

Because, in my highest opinion, a hallmark of someone who knows something is someone who can teach that something to someone else who's up for the challenge of learning that something.

> Reflect On This...
>
> What steps are you going to take to actually get through this? To, in relation to the three Cs, to **commit**? 
>
> People always tell me that they aren't a math person. And my job is to not convince you that math will always be fun. Or that it's "easy".
>
> I also can't close a decades worth of math gaps. So when you're faced with a challenge, what will you ask yourself?
>
> Be self-reflective. Introspective. Have the awareness to actively close those gaps you recognize yourself as you pace yourself through this content. There are many resources out there to help with that, but it starts with **you** taking that step and being okay with recognizing you might need to fix something else before continuing on here. Take initiative and be your own best self. To grow and learn for the sake of improving the self.
>
> Build small successes, enter in with this mindset of improving for yourself (and not for others), and you'll take this a mile. Perservere, because you are a math person; because to be a math person, it's to perservere. I've got your back along the way.
>
> Say this to yourself:
> > ***I am a math person simply because I'm doing math. When I struggle with math, it's not because I am not capable; it's because math is hard. Mathematics does not require a special talent. It requires time, effort, and a willingness to not give up.***
